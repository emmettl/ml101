Three specimen
model cards

A model card is a page that travels with a model: what it is for, what it was trained and tested on, how it scored, for whom it scores worse, and what it must not be used for. Here are three, written for models you can build in this course, with the figures the labs measure. Read them for the shape, then write one for yours.

Eight headings, and why each one is there

Purpose and users

What it is for, and for whom

The task, the people who will act on its answer, and the uses it was never designed for. Most harm comes from a model used for something adjacent to its purpose.

Data and scores

What it saw, and what it got

The training data, the held-out data, and the scores on each, beside a do-nothing baseline. A score without its baseline is lesson 03's trap.

Who it fails

The audit, group by group

The Fairness Lab's table: the same scores for each group the model might treat differently, computed before someone else computes them.

Limits and upkeep

What it cannot do, and when to retrain

Known failures, the conditions it was not tested in, and the Drift Lab's question: how will anyone know when the world has moved?

Three cards for three models from this course

Cancellation early-warning model

The capstone's model, with its sensible settings. Every figure is what the capstone reports for that setup.

Purpose
Flag customers likely to cancel in the coming period, so that the retention team can call them first. Output: a probability per customer, flagged at 30% confidence.
Intended users and uses
The retention team, as a prioritised call list. Not for pricing, credit, or any decision that disadvantages the customer; not for individual judgements without a person in the loop.
Model
One neuron (logistic regression) over three inputs: months as a customer, support tickets, logins last month. Trained by gradient descent, 300 steps. A leaked column, “win-back offer sent”, was identified and excluded because it is filled in only after a cancellation.
Data
600 archived customers, synthetic, 16% of whom cancelled. Split three ways: 360 for training, 120 for tuning the cut-off, 120 scored once at the end. Deployment figures come from 4,000 further synthetic customers.
Scores
  • Held-out (120 customers): accuracy 82%, cancellers caught 50%, flags that were right 41%.
  • Training (360 customers): accuracy 86%, cancellers caught 63%, flags right 56%.
  • On 4,000 new customers: accuracy 83%, cancellers caught 59%, flags right 47%.
  • Do-nothing baseline: 84% accurate, catches 0%. Accuracy is therefore not the headline; cancellers caught is.
Who it fails
Not audited by customer group, because the synthetic data carries no group. A real deployment must run the Fairness Lab's table by region, tenure band and plan before use.
Limits
One snapshot in time. Nothing tests whether a flagged customer can actually be retained, which is the decision the model exists to serve. Half of cancellers are missed at this cut-off; a lower cut-off catches more at the price of more calls.
Upkeep
Cancellations are known about three months after a prediction. Score each month's flags once its labels arrive, and retrain on a schedule; the Drift Lab shows why a monitor on the inputs is not enough.

Character language model, seven chapters of Alice

The neural network from Table vs Network at six characters of context, after 400,000 training characters.

Purpose
Predict the next character of English text in the style of one book, for teaching. It writes fluent-looking nonsense on request.
Intended users and uses
Learners of this course, to see a language model's odds move as it trains. Not for generating text anyone will read as information; it has none.
Model
8-number embeddings for 33 characters, six of them laid side by side, one hidden layer of 64 tanh neurons, softmax output: 5,545 knobs. Trained one character at a time by descent with a decaying learning rate, in the browser.
Data
The first 90% of chapters 1–7 of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, about 73,000 characters, public domain. The last 10% is held out and never trained on.
Scores
  • Surprise per character (lower is better; 3.5 is blind guessing): 1.62 on studied text, about 1.79 on the held-out 10%, a gap of 0.17.
  • The smoothed count table at the same context scores 1.59 held-out and beats the network; at its best context it scores 1.48. This model does not win on raw score, and the lab says so.
  • Recitation: 0% of 600 freshly written characters appear word for word in the book, against 5–26% for the count table at six to eight characters of context.
Who it fails
Not applicable in the usual sense: no people are scored. Its text mirrors one Victorian book, and only that.
Limits
No word-level meaning, no grammar beyond a few characters, no knowledge. Anything it writes that looks like a fact is an accident of spelling.
Upkeep
None: the book does not change. Retrain only to change the teaching demonstration.

Loan approval model, trained on past decisions

The Fairness Lab's model at its default settings: 60% prejudice in the past decisions, and the group column visible. This is the card that should stop a launch.

Purpose
Reproduce the lender's past approval decisions from three columns: test score, neighbourhood, and group (Blue or Orange). Output: approval confidence, approved at 50%.
Intended users and uses
As built, none. The model reproduces prejudice in its training labels and must not be used to decide, rank or pre-screen applicants. It is kept as a teaching specimen.
Model
One neuron, trained 400 steps by descent to agree with 3,000 past decisions. Learned weights: test score +1.50, neighbourhood −0.05, group −0.90. The weight on group is the staff's mark-down, learned.
Data
3,000 invented applicants with past decisions made by staff who marked Orange applicants down; 6,000 fresh applicants for scoring. The invented data records who would truly repay, which real data never does.
Scores
  • Agreement with the past decisions: 79%. This is the only score a real lender could compute, and it rises as the prejudice in the labels rises.
  • Agreement with who would truly repay: 70%, against 76% for a model trained on even-handed decisions.
Who it fails
  • Approved: Blue 53%, Orange 17%.
  • Would repay, and approved: Blue 78%, Orange 31%. A 47-point gap.
  • Approved, and would repay: Blue 77%, Orange 93%. Orange applicants are held to a higher bar.
  • Deleting the group column leaves a 32-point gap, carried by neighbourhood, which predicts group.
Limits
The labels are the problem, not the model, and no held-out score computed against those labels can show it. Two groups of equal size; real cases have many overlapping groups and many proxies.
Upkeep
Not to be maintained: to be replaced by a model trained on repayment outcomes rather than past approvals, with the audit above run on every retrain.
