From toy to ChatGPT

You have now met every idea inside a modern language model: knobs, a loss, descent, layers, tokens, embeddings, attention and next-token prediction. What is left is a matter of size, and of what is done to the model after it has read the internet.

The one idea

The same machine, a billion times larger, then taught how to behave

Nothing in a large language model is a different kind of thing from what you have already trained in this course. It is a stack of attention layers and small networks, reading token embeddings, with all its knobs set by gradient descent to lower its surprise at the next token. The line in lesson 00 had 2 knobs. The largest openly documented model has 405 billion. The update rule is the one from lesson 01.

Size is not the whole story, though. A model that has only learned to continue text is not an assistant. Ask it a question and it may well reply with three more questions, because on the internet questions often come in lists. Turning a text-continuer into something that answers takes two further, much smaller, rounds of training.

A chatbot is a next-token predictor, trained on a vast amount of text and then nudged, with far fewer examples, towards continuing text the way a helpful assistant would.
  1. Pretraining

    Predict the next token across trillions of tokens of books, code and web pages. Almost all the cost and almost all the knowledge come from here.

    Result: fluent, knowledgeable, no manners.
  2. Fine-tuning

    Continue training on tens of thousands of example conversations written by people, showing what a good answer looks like. Same loss, new examples.

    Result: it answers instead of rambling.
  3. Learning from preferences

    People compare pairs of answers. A second model learns to predict which they prefer, and the chatbot's knobs are nudged towards answers it scores highly.

    Result: more helpful, more careful, more agreeable.

The arithmetic, and what it does not buy

Bigger is better at the loss. The loss is not truth.

Training costs roughly six arithmetic operations per knob for every token read, and the rule of thumb is to feed a model about twenty tokens per knob. So a model ten times larger wants ten times the text, and costs a hundred times as much to train. The return on that spend has been remarkably reliable: the loss falls smoothly and predictably as models grow, and abilities nobody programmed, such as translation, arithmetic and writing code, show up along the way.

Now recall what the loss measures: surprise at the next token of human-written text. Every stage above rewards producing what a plausible, approved-of reply would look like. None of them checks whether it is true.

Hallucination

Asked for a reference that does not exist, the most plausible continuation is a well-formatted reference. The model is doing its job. See hallucination.

Frozen in time

The knobs stop moving when training ends. It knows nothing later, and learns nothing from talking to you, unless text is placed in its context window.

A mirror of its text

Lesson 05's warning at full size: whatever regularities, skills and prejudices are in the training text are in the model.

Not only language

Image generators use the same knobs, loss and descent. Their task is removing noise from a picture a little at a time, instead of predicting a next token.

Takeaways

What to remember, from the whole course

It is knobs all the way up

Two of them or 405 billion, set by stepping downhill on a loss.

Examples are the only teacher

What a model can do, and what it gets wrong, both trace back to its data.

Only unseen data tells you if it learned

From a 13-knob curve to a chatbot reciting a book, memorising looks like success.

Fluent is not the same as right

The loss rewards plausible text. Checking it remains your job.
