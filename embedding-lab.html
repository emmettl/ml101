Token & Embedding Lab

Two machines that sit in front of every language model. The first cuts text into pieces from a fixed list. The second gives each piece a position, learned from nothing but which words appear together. Watch both being built, then ask the second one a question in arithmetic.

Scope: a byte-pair tokeniser learned from seven chapters of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (up to 400 merges; real ones use tens of thousands), and skip-gram embeddings with negative sampling trained on an invented mini-language of 900 short sentences about 40 words. The mini-language is built so that rank, gender and age are genuinely present; real text is far messier, and real embeddings also absorb its prejudices. Subword embeddings, positional information and contextual embeddings are omitted.

    The model never sees your words

    It sees whatever the tokeniser hands it. An unusual name or a typo arrives as a heap of fragments, which is why models handle them worse than common words.

    Meaning needs room

    Rank, gender, age, animal and food are five independent things to keep apart. Two coordinates cannot hold them all, so something gives. Eight can.

    The map inherits the text

    Positions come only from which words keep company. If the text pairs “nurse” with “she”, so will the geometry. Embeddings are a mirror, including of things you would prefer they were not.

    Going further

    Byte-pair encoding for text is from Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, “Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units” (2016). The embedding method is word2vec: Mikolov et al., “Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality” (2013), which also introduced the king − man + woman example. On bias, see Bolukbasi et al., “Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?” (2016). Analogy arithmetic is more fragile on real text than on this tidy mini-language.
