Vision Lab

Tiny pictures of four shapes, drawn at random places with noise, and two networks trained to name them. One wires every pixel to every neuron. The other slides a few small filters across the picture and uses the same nine knobs at every position. Train both, look at what they learned, and move the shapes.

Scope: 12×12 pictures of a bar, a cross, a ring and a corner, 400 for training and 200 of each kind for testing. The convolutional network has one layer of 3×3 filters, ReLU, and takes each filter's strongest response anywhere in the picture (max pooling) before a final layer. Real networks stack many such layers and pool step by step; the principle is this one. Training runs on the page for 1,200 steps of 16 pictures, which takes a second or two.

      Sharing knobs is knowing where you are does not matter

      One filter, applied at every position, is a promise: what counts as a corner here counts as a corner there. The fully connected network has to learn that separately for every place a corner could be, and with 400 pictures it cannot.

      Fewer knobs, and better

      Eight filters are 116 knobs. Thirty-two fully connected neurons are 4,772. The small one wins on moved shapes by a wide margin, because it is built for the thing that varies.

      Look at the filters

      The learned filters are edge and corner detectors that nobody designed. In a deep network the next layer combines them into parts, and the layer after that into objects. Same idea, stacked.

      Going further

      Convolutional networks with learned filters begin with LeCun et al., “Backpropagation Applied to Handwritten Zip Code Recognition” (1989). The moment they took over vision was Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, “ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks” (2012). To see real learned filters, Zeiler and Fergus, “Visualizing and Understanding Convolutional Networks” (2013), draws them layer by layer.
