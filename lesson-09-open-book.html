Lesson 09 · Using a model

Open-book answers

Lesson 08 ended with a model whose knobs froze on the day training stopped, and which would sooner invent an answer than admit it has none. The most widely used remedy involves no training at all. Look the answer up first, and put what you find in front of the model.

The one idea

A model can use what is in its knobs, or what is on its desk

Everything a language model knows is in one of two places. The first is its knobs, set during training: vast, blurry, impossible to edit, and out of date from the day training ended. The second is its context window, the text it is reading right now: small, exact, and entirely up to you. Lesson 06 showed that attention lets every token consult every other token in that window. So anything you place there, the model can read and quote.

An exam taken from memory and an exam taken with the textbook open are different exams. The second one depends far less on what you happened to memorise and far more on whether you can find the right page. That is the whole idea.

Retrieval-augmented generation: search your documents for the passages most likely to hold the answer, paste them into the prompt above the question, and ask the model to answer from them. No knob moves.
  1. Cut and index

    Ahead of time, cut every document into passages and turn each passage into a list of numbers, as lesson 05 did for words.

    Done once, redone when a document changes.
  2. Search

    Turn the question into numbers the same way. Keep the few passages whose numbers point most nearly the same direction.

    Milliseconds, and no language model involved.
  3. Hand over

    Build a prompt: an instruction, the passages, the question. The model reads and answers, and can say which passage it used.

    The only step that needs the model.

The mechanism

The search decides what the model gets to know

The book below is the one from lesson 07, cut into 350 overlapping passages of 60 words. Each passage has been turned into a list of numbers with one slot per word in the book: a slot is large if the word is frequent in this passage and rare in the book as a whole. The question gets the same treatment, and passages are ranked by how closely their list lines up with the question's, which is the arrow-alignment score from lesson 06. This is the simplest embedding there is. It knows nothing about meaning, and that is about to matter.

    Try all four questions. The first is found at once, because it uses the book's own words. The second needs two passages. The third needs six: the Hatter and the March Hare appear on many pages, and five of those outrank the one with the answer. The fourth is a perfectly good question that fails outright: the book says “Rabbit” and “watch”, never “bunny” or “timepiece”, and a search that matches words has nothing to go on. Production systems replace the word-weights with a learned embedding of whole passages: a transformer (lesson 06) that reads the words in context and is trained to place a question beside the passage that answers it. The rest of the pipeline stays exactly as you see it. Word vectors of the kind you trained in lesson 05 are not enough by themselves. They do know that “bunny” sits beside “rabbit”, and the lab lets you measure how little that turns out to be worth, then switch to a real passage encoder, which finds the passage about the bunny's timepiece that no word search could.

    The arithmetic, and what it does not buy

    Every setting is a trade, and grounded is not the same as true

    The words you hand over are passage length times the number of passages. Raise either and more answers get through. But every word is read by the model on every question, which costs money and time, and an answer that is one sentence in three thousand words is easier to overlook than one in a hundred and fifty. Why not paste in the whole library? Because a context window is finite, the bill is per word, and models attend unevenly to very long prompts.

    Why not train the documents into the knobs instead? Because fine-tuning is slow, costly, has to be redone whenever a document changes, and blurs facts as readily as it stores them. As a rule of thumb, fine-tuning changes how a model behaves, and retrieval changes what it knows today.

    Cut in two

    A passage boundary falls in the middle of the answer, so no passage holds it. The usual fix is to let passages overlap.

    Different words

    The question and the document describe the same thing in different vocabulary. This is what learned passage embeddings are for, and they do not remove the problem entirely.

    Nothing to find

    The answer is not in the library. Search still returns its best matches, and a model may build a confident reply from irrelevant text unless told it may say “I cannot tell”.

    Faithful to a bad source

    Retrieval makes the model repeat your documents. If the document is out of date or wrong, so is the answer, now with a citation.

    Check your understanding

    Knobs, or desk?

    A support assistant built this way tells customers the refund window is 30 days. The company changed it to 14 days last month. What needs to change?

    Ask where the number 30 is coming from.

    The same assistant replies “I can't find anything about that” to “How do I get my money back?”. The refund policy is in the index. Where do you look first?

    The model can only be as good as the passages it was handed.

    Takeaways

    What to remember

    Knobs or desk

    A model knows what it was trained on and what you put in front of it. Only the second can be edited.

    Search sets the ceiling

    If the right passage is not handed over, no model can answer. Debug the search before blaming the model.

    Tune it like any other model

    Passage length, overlap and count are hyperparameters. Keep a set of questions with known answers and measure.

    Grounded is not the same as true

    The answer is as good as the document behind it, and the model may still read it wrongly. Ask for the source, and check it.
