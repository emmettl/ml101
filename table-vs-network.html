Lab 10 · Language models

Table vs Network

Lesson 07 predicted the next character by counting. Here the same job is given to a small neural network, built from the pieces of lessons 03 to 05 and trained while you watch. On one small book the counting table is hard to beat, and this lab does not pretend otherwise. Watch instead for what each machine does when it is given more context than the book can support.

Scope: both models read the first 90% of seven chapters of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland and are scored on the last 10%, which neither has seen. The table is smoothed (Witten–Bell interpolation), so that an unseen continuation is unlikely and not impossible; without that it could not be scored at all. The network gives each of 33 characters 8 learned numbers, lays the last few characters side by side, and passes them through one hidden layer of 64 neurons. It is trained one character at a time by plain gradient descent. Real language models use attention in place of “side by side”, word pieces in place of characters, and about a billion times more of everything.

      Too few knobs to cheat

      A few thousand numbers cannot hold a book, so the network is forced to find regularities. Its two scores stay close however much context you give it. That is generalisation, enforced by a shortage of room.

      On a small book, counting wins

      At its best context the table beats this network outright. Neural language models only pulled ahead once text was counted in millions of words, and they were a curiosity for a decade before that.

      Only one of them scales

      Each extra character of context adds 512 knobs to the network and multiplies the table's possible entries by 33. Give both a thousand times more text and only one of them is still a machine you can build.

      Going further

      This network is a miniature of Bengio et al., “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model” (2003), which reported a clear but modest gain over the best count models on about a million words. On how strong well-smoothed count models are, see Chen and Goodman, “An Empirical Study of Smoothing Techniques for Language Modeling” (1998). Andrej Karpathy's makemore builds the same progression in Python, from counts to a transformer.
