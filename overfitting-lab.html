Overfitting Lab

The upper chart is one curve at one flexibility. The lower chart is the same experiment run at every flexibility at once, scored twice: on the points the curve trained on, and on 200 it never saw. The gap between those two lines is the whole subject.

Scope: one input, a polynomial curve of adjustable degree fitted by least squares, synthetic examples from a fixed smooth wave plus noise, and a single fixed held-out set of 200 points. Here flexibility means polynomial degree; in a neural network it would be layers and units, and the curve can look different. Cross-validation, early stopping and the behaviour of very large networks are omitted.

The gap is the symptom

Two poor scores that agree mean the model is too stiff. A brilliant training score and a poor held-out one mean it is memorising. You can diagnose either without seeing the curve.

Capacity is relative to data

Thirteen knobs is reckless with 14 points and harmless with 60. “Too big” never describes a model alone, only a model and its data together.

A penalty is a preference

Charging for large knob values says: among curves that fit about equally well, prefer the calm one. Push it too far and the curve goes stiff again.

Going further

The trade-off shown here is the bias–variance decomposition; see James et al., An Introduction to Statistical Learning, section 2.2, or Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, chapter 7. Very large neural networks can behave differently, with held-out error falling a second time beyond the point of memorisation; see Belkin et al., “Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias–variance trade-off”, PNAS 2019. That does not rescue the 13-knob curve here.
