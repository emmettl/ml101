Reward Lab

A grid, a start, a goal, and a cliff along the bottom. Every move costs one point, reaching the goal pays twenty, and a step off the cliff costs a hundred and sends the agent back to the start. Nobody tells it which moves were good. It works that out from the rewards, one trip at a time, and the shortest route runs along the edge.

Scope: the cliff walk from Sutton and Barto's textbook, eight squares by five. The agent's knobs are a table of guesses, one per square and direction, nudged after every move; a real agent playing a game or driving a robot replaces the table with a network, and the nudge is the same. Every number here comes from a run you can repeat, and the “Which run” knob shows how much luck is in it.

Credit travels backwards, one square a visit

The goal's reward is nine moves from the start. Each visit to a square copies a little of the next square's guess into its own, so good news spreads back along the route a step at a time. That is why early trips are dreadful and why the table needs many.

Two agents, two routes

Q-learning judges a move as if every later move will be perfect, so it prizes the edge. SARSA judges it by what it will actually do next, random moves included, so it keeps its distance. The first has the better map; the second has the better life.

The reward is the specification

Change what pays and the agent changes what it does, with no regard for what you meant. Every reward function is a contract read by a very literal lawyer, which is the whole difficulty of lesson 08's third stage.

Going further

The cliff walk and both algorithms are from Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd ed., 2018), example 6.6. Q-learning is Watkins (1989); the version with a network in place of the table that learned to play Atari games is Mnih et al., “Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning” (2015). On rewards that were read too literally, see Krakovna et al.'s catalogue of specification gaming.
