Deep dive · using a model someone else trained

Which way
to use a model?

Most people who use machine learning will never train a model from scratch. They will take one that exists and get it to do a job. There are four ways to do that, they fix different things, and they cost very different amounts. Two questions choose between them, and one sum says what the choice will cost each month.

Start here

Four ways, and what each one changes

Prompt

Change what you ask

Instructions and a few examples, sent with every request. Fixes the manner of an answer. Costs nothing to set up and something on every call. Cannot add a fact or a skill.

Retrieve

Change what it can read

Search your documents and hand the model the page, as in lesson 09. Fixes knowledge, especially knowledge that changes. The search sets the ceiling.

Fine-tune

Change how it behaves

Continue training on your examples, as in lesson 08's second stage. Fixes a manner or a narrow skill, given thousands of examples. Blurs facts as readily as it stores them.

Train

Build your own

For a narrow task with a mountain of data, a model built for it alone: usually lesson 10's trees, sometimes a small network. Faster, cheaper, and entirely yours; knows nothing else.

The prompt changes the question. Retrieval changes the reading. Fine-tuning changes the behaviour. Training changes everything.

Before you ship

Six questions worth answering first

  1. What does a right answer look like, and who says so?

    Write down fifty questions with answers you would accept. This is the held-out set of lesson 02, and without it every other question has no number attached.

  2. What happens when it is wrong?

    Lesson 08: plausible is not the same as true. Decide who checks, what is shown to the user, and whether “I cannot tell” is an allowed answer. It should be.

  3. Where does the knowledge live, and who can change it?

    In the knobs it is frozen; in an index it is editable; in the prompt it is yours. Lesson 09's support bot with a stale refund policy is the case to keep in mind.

  4. What goes into the prompt, and should it?

    Everything sent is read, stored by someone, and billed. Send the passages that matter, not the document; the customer's question, not their record.

  5. How will you know it has drifted?

    The Drift Lab's lesson: inputs can be watched today, answers only once someone has judged them. Sample and grade a slice every week, against the same fifty questions.

  6. Who is treated worse?

    The Fairness Lab's audit is one group-by. Run it on the questions and on the people asking, before someone else does.

Common traps

Six confusions worth keeping apart

Fine-tuning ≠ teaching facts

It moves the manner and the skill into the knobs. Facts go in blurred and come out stale. Retrieval is for facts.

A long context ≠ retrieval

Pasting the whole document in works, costs twenty times the tokens on every request, and models read very long prompts unevenly. Search first.

A demo ≠ an evaluation

Ten good answers you chose to look at are lesson 02's training set. Fifty questions you wrote before you looked are the test.

Cheaper per token ≠ cheaper

A small model that needs three tries, or a long prompt to behave, can cost more than a large one that gets it right in one.

The model's confidence ≠ yours

A fluent answer and a correct one look the same on the page. Only checking against something outside the model tells them apart.

Trained on your data ≠ yours

A fine-tuned hosted model still lives with the host. Ask what is retained, who can see the prompts, and how to leave.
