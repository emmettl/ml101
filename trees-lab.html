Trees Lab

A model made of if-then questions, on the same four puzzles the Network Playground used. Nothing here is trained by descent: each question is chosen greedily as the split that best sorts the examples. Grow one tree and read it. Grow it deep and watch it memorise. Then grow a hundred, and let them vote or take turns.

Scope: 320 training examples and 80 unseen, with a share of the training labels flipped so that there is something to memorise. Two inputs only, so a tree's boxes can be drawn; real trees ask about hundreds of columns and a forest also gives each tree a random subset of them. Boosting here takes a fixed step of 0.3 per tree; real libraries add regularisation, early stopping and a great deal of engineering, which change the numbers and not the picture.

The tree

Greedy, and readable

A tree never turns a knob. It picks the best question it can see right now, then the best in each branch, and stops. That makes it fast, easy to explain, and blind to any split that only pays off two questions later.

Memorising has a new shape

Lesson 02's wiggly curve becomes a boundary of tiny boxes, one per stray example. The cure is the same: less capacity, or more data, or an average over many models that each memorised differently.

Still the champions of the spreadsheet

On tables of columns, boosted trees beat neural networks more often than not, and they train in seconds. Networks win on pixels, sound and text, where the columns have no meaning on their own.

Going further

Trees grown this way are from Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, Classification and Regression Trees (1984). Forests are Breiman, “Random Forests” (2001). Boosting as descent with a tree for a step is Friedman, “Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine” (2001); the library everyone uses is Chen and Guestrin, “XGBoost” (2016). On why trees still win on tables, see Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux, “Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on tabular data?” (2022).
