Neuron Lab

A neuron does not say yes or no. It says how sure it is. Somebody still has to decide how sure is sure enough, and that choice moves errors from one kind to the other without removing any. The lower chart lines every example up by the neuron's confidence so you can see exactly which ones change sides.

Scope: a single sigmoid neuron with two inputs and three knobs, trained by mini-batch gradient descent on cross-entropy, scored on the 160 examples it trained on. Triangles are the class being looked for. Held-out scoring, class imbalance, calibration and the cost of each kind of error are left out; the last of those is what decides the cut-off in practice.

The cut-off is a policy, not a fact

Training places the line. It does not tell you how cautious to be. A missed tumour and a needless biopsy do not cost the same, and no loss function knows that unless someone says so.

Accuracy hides the trade

Several rows in the table have nearly the same accuracy and very different errors. Always ask which kind of mistake a reported accuracy is made of.

Some overlap cannot be trained away

Where circles and triangles genuinely mix, no line and no cut-off separates them. That is lesson 00's error floor, in a classification problem.

Going further

A single sigmoid neuron trained on cross-entropy is logistic regression; see James et al., An Introduction to Statistical Learning, chapter 4. The last two columns of the table are precision and recall; sweeping the cut-off across all values traces the ROC curve. The limit on what one such unit can represent is from Minsky and Papert, Perceptrons (1969).
