Deep dive · the names that trip everyone up

Which knob?
Which data?

Machine learning has two families of names that sound alike and mean opposite things. Parameter and hyperparameter. Training, validation and test. Each family is decoded by a single question. Learn the two questions and the vocabulary stops being a wall.

Start here

Two questions replace seven definitions

Question one · for any number

Who sets it?

If gradient descent moves it during training, it is a parameter. If a person chooses it, before or around training, it is a hyperparameter. That is the entire distinction.

Question two · for any pile of data

What is it allowed to influence?

Training data may move the parameters. Validation data may inform a person's choices. Test data may influence nothing at all. It exists to be looked at once.

A parameter is learned. A hyperparameter is chosen. And the moment a pile of data influences a choice, it stops being a test.

Training

The examples descent learns from.

  • Moves the parameters, thousands of times.
  • Its score always flatters, and can only improve with capacity.
  • Tells you whether training worked, not whether the model is any good.
Usually about 10–20%

Validation

The examples you consult while making choices.

  • Compares learning rates, sizes, cut-offs, when to stop.
  • Honest at first, and a little less so each time you consult it.
  • Also called a development or held-out set, which is where confusion starts.
Usually about 10–20%

Test

The examples nobody looks at until the end.

  • Influences nothing. Scored once, reported as is.
  • The only number that estimates how the model does on new data.
  • If it disappoints, you do not get to try again on it.

When someone shows you a score

Five questions worth asking

  1. Scored on which pile? “Accuracy” with no pile named is nearly always training accuracy.
  2. How many things were tried against that pile? One honest look and two hundred tries produce very different numbers.
  3. Compared with what? Doing nothing, a simple rule, or last year's model. A score alone means little.
  4. Could any input contain the answer? Anything recorded after the event being predicted is the answer in disguise.
  5. Does the test data look like tomorrow's data? A random slice of last year tells you about last year.

Common traps

Six distinctions worth keeping separate

Parameter ≠ hyperparameter

“The model has 70 billion parameters” counts learned knobs. The learning rate is not one of them, however much it mattered.

Validation ≠ test

Many papers and libraries call the validation set “test”. Ask what it influenced. If the answer is “our choices”, it was validation.

Loss ≠ accuracy

Loss is what descent pushes down, and it has a slope. Accuracy is what people quote, and it does not. They usually move together. Not always.

Epoch ≠ step

A step is one update from one batch. An epoch is one pass through all the training data, which may be thousands of steps.

Training ≠ inference

Training moves the knobs and is done once, at great cost. Inference uses the frozen knobs to answer a question, and is what you pay for per request.

Model ≠ algorithm

The algorithm is the procedure that learns. The model is what it leaves behind: a recipe plus the numbers it settled on.
