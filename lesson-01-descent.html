Lesson 01 · How learning happens

Rolling downhill

You fitted a line by nudging two knobs and watching one number. A computer does the same, except it never has to guess which way to nudge, and it can do it for a billion knobs at once.

The one idea

Feel the slope under your feet, take a step downhill, repeat

Imagine every possible setting of the two knobs laid out as a map, with the height of the ground at each spot equal to the miss you would get there. Bad settings are hillsides. The best setting is the bottom of a valley. Training is walking to the bottom in fog: you cannot see the valley, but you can always feel which way the ground slopes where you stand.

For each knob the computer asks one question: if I turned this a hair, would the miss go up or down, and how steeply? The answers, one per knob, are the gradient. It points uphill. So step the other way.

The direction comes free from the slope. The only thing you have to choose is how far to step.
Gradient

One number per knob: how fast the miss rises if that knob is turned up.

Learning rate

How far to move each knob per step, as a multiple of the slope. The single most consequential setting in training.

Step

Measure the slope, move every knob a little downhill. Then do it again.

Convergence

The steps have become so small that nothing is changing. You are at the bottom.

The failure at the top is worth a second look. Every one of those steps pointed the right way. The stride was simply so long that each step sailed across the valley and landed higher up the far side than where it started. From there the slope is steeper, so the next step is longer still. Engineers call this divergence, and a training run that suddenly reports “not a number” for its miss has usually done exactly this.

The arithmetic

One step, written out

Suppose the line is currently too low for most examples. Turning the lift knob up by a hair would reduce the miss, so the slope for that knob is negative: say −6. With a learning rate of 0.05 the update is

new lift = old lift − 0.05 × (−6) = old lift + 0.3

That is the entire algorithm. There is no search and no cleverness: subtract a small multiple of the slope, for every knob, again and again. When a model has a hundred billion knobs the sum is the same, done a hundred billion times per step.

Why use only a few examples per step?

Measuring the slope on every example is accurate and slow. A random handful gives a rough slope for a fraction of the cost. The path jitters but gets there far sooner per unit of computing. This is stochastic gradient descent, and it is how every large model is trained.

Why not just solve for the bottom?

For a straight line you can, with one formula. For anything with layers there is no formula, only the slope underfoot. Descent is what remains.

Takeaways

What to remember

The slope gives the direction

Nobody searches for good knob settings. The gradient says which way is down.

You choose the stride

Too small wastes the budget. Too large overshoots, and then it runs away.

Rough slopes are good enough

Small random batches trade a jittery path for many more steps per second.

It is only subtraction

knob ← knob − rate × slope. Every model in this course is trained this way.
