Descent Lab

The map is the landscape from the Line Fitter. This time nobody drags the dot. It feels the slope, steps downhill, and repeats. The interesting part is what the path looks like as you lengthen the stride, right up to the point where it stops working.

Scope: plain gradient descent, with optional mini-batches, on a two-knob straight-line model with squared misses and a fixed starting point. This valley has a single bottom, so descent always finds it if the stride is safe. Momentum, adaptive step sizes such as Adam, learning-rate schedules and landscapes with many valleys are omitted.

What does one step actually compute?

Each row is the rule from the lesson: new knob = old knob − rate × slope. Watch the slopes shrink as the walk nears the bottom, and the steps shrink with them.

Steps shrink on their own

The stride is a multiple of the slope, and the slope flattens near the bottom. Descent slows down exactly where it should, with no one telling it to.

The valley is narrow one way, long the other

A stride that is safe along the valley can be too long across it. That is the zig-zag you see, and the reason a single learning rate is always a compromise.

The edge has no warning

Just under the largest safe rate the walk is at its fastest. Just over, it is destroyed. In practice people start lower than they need to and creep up.

Going further

Gradient descent goes back to Cauchy in 1847. The mini-batch form used to train modern networks is described in Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, Deep Learning, chapter 8, which also covers the refinements left out here (momentum, RMSProp, Adam). The divergence threshold shown in this lab is exact only for a bowl-shaped loss like this one; for deep networks it has to be found by experiment.
