Lesson 03 · Classification

From numbers to decisions

So far the machine has guessed a number. Most useful questions want a verdict instead: spam or not, tumour or not, approve or decline. One small addition turns the straight line into a decision-maker, and it has a name you have heard: a neuron.

The one idea

Draw a line, then say how sure you are

Take two measurements of something, say an email's length and how many links it contains, and plot every email as a dot. If spam tends to sit in one region, a straight line between the regions is a usable spam filter: everything on one side is flagged.

A hard line is a crude answer, though. A dot just over the line is not as clearly spam as one far beyond it. So the machine does two things. It computes how far the dot is from the line, exactly as in lesson 00: multiply each input by a knob, add them up, add one more knob. Then it passes that distance through a squashing curve that turns any number into a value between 0 and 1. Far on one side gives nearly 1, far on the other gives nearly 0, and on the line itself gives 0.5. Read it as a probability.

A neuron is a weighted sum followed by a squish. The weights place the line. The squish turns distance from the line into confidence.

On the opposite-corners pattern no setting works, and training does no better than you did. This is not a shortage of effort or data. One neuron draws one straight line, and no single straight line separates opposite corners. The pattern is called XOR, and in 1969 this observation helped stall neural-network research for a decade. The fix turned out to be simple. It is the next lesson.

The arithmetic

Scoring a probability

Squared misses suit quantities. For probabilities there is a better score. If the right answer was “spam” and the neuron said 0.9, it was barely surprised; if it said 0.01, it was very surprised. The loss is the average surprise, measured as the negative logarithm of the probability given to the right answer. Its proper name is cross-entropy.

Said 0.9, was right

Surprise 0.11. Fine.

Said 0.5, was right

Surprise 0.69. A coin flip earns this on every example.

Said 0.01, was right

Surprise 4.6. Confident and wrong is punished hardest of all.

Why not just count mistakes?

A count has no slope: nudging a knob slightly usually changes no verdicts, so descent has nothing to follow. Surprise changes smoothly.

Takeaways

What to remember

A neuron is lesson 00 plus a squish

Weighted sum, then a curve that turns it into a probability.

The output is confidence, not a verdict

Where to cut it into yes and no is a separate decision, made by a person.

Train on surprise, not on mistakes

Cross-entropy has a slope everywhere, so descent can follow it.

One neuron, one line

Anything that needs a bent boundary needs more than one neuron.
