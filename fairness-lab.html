Lab 12 · Bias

Fairness Lab

A model is trained to shrink its error on the examples it is given. If those examples are past decisions, and the past was prejudiced, the smallest error belongs to the model that repeats the prejudice. This lab builds that model, shows why deleting the sensitive column does not cure it, and then shows that “fair” has three reasonable meanings which cannot all hold at once.

Scope: the applicants are invented, which is the only reason the lab can know who would really have repaid. A real lender sees repayment only for the people it approved, and cannot compute the “would repay” figures on this page. There are two groups of equal size, called Blue and Orange so that you bring no assumptions to them. The model is the single neuron of lesson 03, trained on 3,000 past decisions and measured on 6,000 new applicants. Real cases involve many groups that overlap, many proxies at once, and harm that a number does not capture.

      The loss cannot see it

      Scored against the past decisions, the prejudiced model is the accurate one. Nothing in training, validation or testing will flag it, because all three are measured against the same labels. Someone has to go looking, group by group.

      Deleting the column is not a cure

      Anything correlated with group can stand in for it: a postcode, a first name, a school, a gap in employment. And once the column is gone you can no longer run the audit that would show the harm.

      Fair is a choice, and someone must make it

      When groups differ in how often the outcome really occurs, equal approval rates, equal chances for those who qualify, and approvals that mean the same thing cannot all hold. Which to keep is a question about values, and the model has none.

      Going further

      That the three measures cannot all be satisfied is proved in Chouldechova, “Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact” (2017), and Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, “Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores” (2016). Separate cut-offs come from Hardt, Price and Srebro, “Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning” (2016). The word-vector measurement follows Bolukbasi et al., “Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?” (2016); the vectors are GloVe (Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014, public domain). Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan's free textbook Fairness and Machine Learning covers what this lab leaves out, which is most of the subject.
