Lesson 10 · Other machines

Questions instead of knobs

Every model so far has been a machine with knobs, set by rolling downhill. Here is one with no knobs at all. It asks a question about the input, then another, then another, and stops at an answer. It cannot be trained by descent, it can be printed and read, and a hundred of them together still beat neural networks on most spreadsheets.

The one idea

Sort the examples with the best question you can see

A decision tree is a flowchart. At the top is a question about one input: is the customer's tenure below eight months? Each answer leads to another question, or to a leaf that gives the verdict. To make a prediction you start at the top and follow the answers down.

Building one is greedy. Look at every input, and every place you could cut it, and ask: which single cut sorts the examples most cleanly into their two classes? Take that cut. Now do the same again on each side, with only the examples that landed there. Stop when the sides are pure, or too small, or you have asked enough questions. There is no loss to roll down and no gradient. Each question is chosen once and never revisited, which is why a tree is fast to build and why it can miss a split that would only have paid off two questions later.

A tree is a stack of if-then questions, each chosen as the single split that best sorts the examples it sees. What descent did for knobs, greedy search does for questions.
Depth

How many questions can be asked on the way to an answer. Depth is the tree's capacity, and plays exactly the role polynomial degree played in lesson 02.

Boxes

Every question is about one input, so every cut is parallel to an axis. A tree's boundary is a set of boxes, and a diagonal line takes it a staircase of questions to approximate.

Readable

A tree with a dozen questions can be printed on a page and argued with. Nothing else in this course can.

No scaling needed

Only the order of values matters to a question, so a column in pounds and a column in millions need no rescaling, and a wild outlier moves nothing.

At depth twelve, on the ring, the tree gets nearly every training example right and does worse on unseen ones than it did at depth four. It has drawn a private box around each mislabelled point. That is overfitting, and trees are unusually good at it, because a question costs nothing and there is always another to ask.

The remedy, and why it works

Many trees: voting, or taking turns

A single deep tree memorises. The cure that made trees the workhorse of applied machine learning is to grow many and combine them, in one of two ways.

  1. A forest votes

    Grow each tree on a reshuffled copy of the data, drawn with replacement, so that each memorises different accidents. Average their verdicts and the accidents cancel.

    Hard to overfit; most of the gain comes from the first few dozen trees.
  2. Boosting takes turns

    Grow a small tree. Look at what it got wrong. Grow the next tree on those residuals, add a fraction of it, and repeat. The sum of the trees is the model.

    It is gradient descent, with a tree for a step. Add too many and it memorises too.
  3. Where they win

    On tables of named columns, boosted trees beat neural networks more often than not, in seconds, on a laptop. On pixels, sound and text, where no column means anything on its own, the network wins.

    The reason to know both.

Boosting with depth-1 trees, each a single question, has a limit worth seeing in the lab: a sum of one-input answers cannot express “both inputs have the same sign”, so it never solves the opposite-corners puzzle, however many trees you add. Give each tree a second question and it does.

Boxes and votes

A tree's decision boundary is always made of edges parallel to the axes, never a diagonal. Why?

Think about what one question can refer to.

On the ring, a depth-12 tree with 46 questions scores 93% on its 320 training examples and 78% on unseen ones. A forest of fifty such trees scores 94% and 85%. What did the forest change?

Each tree in the forest is just as deep, and just as greedy.

Takeaways

What to remember

Not everything is descent

A tree is built by greedy search over questions. Same loss to shrink, no gradient in sight.

Depth is capacity

A deep tree memorises like a high-degree polynomial, and the held-out score catches it the same way.

Many weak, one strong

Forests average away accidents; boosting fixes them in turn. Both turn a fragile model into a robust one.

Pick the machine for the data

Tables of columns: trees, usually boosted. Pixels, sound, text: networks. Knowing which is most of the job.
