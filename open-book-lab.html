Lab 11 · Using a model

Open-Book Lab

A language model can only answer from its knobs or from the text in front of it. Retrieval chooses that text. Here a book is cut into passages and searched, and 24 questions with known answers measure how often the right passage reaches the model. When it does not, no model, however large, can put that right.

Scope: the book is seven chapters of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland. There is no language model on this page: the lab does the retrieval half for real, shows exactly what would be sent, and checks against an answer key whether the answer is in it. By default passages and questions are turned into numbers by weighting words (a word counts for more if it is rare in the book). Two further settings bring in a learned embedding of words: GloVe, 50 numbers per word, trained on six billion words of Wikipedia and news. Production systems go one step further and embed whole passages with a transformer trained for the purpose, which is too large to run here; the lab lets you measure why they had to. Every other step, and every failure you can produce here, is the same in production.

    The model never changed
    
          
    
            Nothing here trained anything. Retrieval adds knowledge by pasting
            text into the prompt, which is why it can be updated in seconds, can
            cite its source, and costs nothing like fine-tuning.
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Retrieval sets the ceiling
    
          
    
            If the passage is not handed over, the best possible reply is “I
            cannot tell”. Most failures blamed on the model in these systems are
            search failures, and you find them with an answer key, as you did
            here.
          
    
        
    
        
    
          
    Every setting is a trade
    
          
    
            Longer passages and more of them find more answers and bury each one
            deeper in text the model must read and you must pay for. Passage
            length, overlap and count are hyperparameters, tuned on held-out
            questions like any other.
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Going further
    
        
    
          The name comes from Lewis et al.,
          “Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP
            Tasks”
          (2020). The word-weighting used here is TF-IDF, from Spärck Jones
          (1972); its descendant BM25 remains a hard baseline to beat, and
          production systems often run it alongside a learned embedding and
          merge the two rankings. On how models use long contexts unevenly, see
          Liu et al.,
          “Lost in the Middle”
          (2023). The word vectors are GloVe, from Pennington, Socher and
          Manning,
          “Global Vectors for Word Representation”
          (2014), released into the public domain; the lab ships the 12,000 or
          so words it can need, at one byte per number. The passage encoders
          that did overtake word matching began with Karpukhin et al.,
          “Dense Passage Retrieval”
          (2020).
