Lesson 12 · Other machines

The same knobs everywhere

A picture is a grid of numbers. Wire every one of them to every neuron, as lesson 04 would, and the network has to learn what a cat looks like in the top left, and again in the middle, and again everywhere else. The fix is one of the best ideas in the field: learn a small detector once, and slide it across the whole picture.

The one idea

A filter is nine knobs used at every position

Take a 3×3 patch of weights, a filter. Lay it on the top-left corner of the picture, multiply each of its nine numbers by the pixel underneath, and add them up: one number, saying how much that patch looks like the filter. Slide the filter one pixel to the right and do it again. Do it at every position and you have a new, slightly smaller picture, a map of where the pattern occurs. That sliding is convolution.

A convolutional network is lesson 04's network with one change in the wiring: instead of every neuron seeing every pixel with its own weight, a neuron sees a 3×3 patch, and every neuron in the layer shares the same nine weights. The knobs are shared, so there are far fewer of them, and what the filter detects at one place it detects at every place. Train it by exactly the descent and backpropagation you already know.

Convolution: slide a small filter over the picture, multiplying and summing at each position. Sharing one filter's knobs across all positions is what lets a network recognise a shape wherever it appears.
Feature map

The picture a filter produces: bright where the pattern is, dark where it is not. One map per filter; a layer has many.

Pooling

Summarising a map: “how strongly did this filter fire, anywhere?” Taking the largest value throws away position, which is the point.

Stacking

A second layer of filters reads the first layer's maps, so it detects patterns of patterns: edges become corners, corners become parts, parts become things.

Not only pictures

Anything with a grid or a line has neighbours: sound, text, sensor readings. The same trick applies, and did, before attention took over for text.

The arithmetic, and what it does not buy

Fewer knobs, and the right kind of ignorance

A fully connected neuron looking at a 12×12 picture has 144 weights; thirty-two of them plus an output layer come to 4,772 knobs. Eight 3×3 filters plus an output layer come to 116. The lab trains both on four shapes drawn at random positions, and the small one wins, not by a little: moved shapes that the big network scores near blind guessing on, the filters name almost every time.

The reason is not cleverness but built-in ignorance. Sharing a filter across positions is a decision, made before training, that where a pattern is does not change what it is. For pictures that is nearly always right, and every training picture then teaches the network about every position at once. Where it is wrong, the same decision hurts: a filter cannot tell a face from an upside-down face without being shown both, and it has no idea that the sky is usually at the top.

Takeaways

What to remember

Slide, multiply, sum

A filter is a few knobs applied at every position. Its output is a map of where its pattern occurs.

Shared knobs, shared knowledge

Learn a detector once and it works everywhere. Fewer knobs, less data, and shapes recognised wherever they appear.

Built-in assumptions are the point

Convolution assumes position does not change identity. Where that holds it wins; where it does not, it is blind.

Stacked, it sees things

Edges, then corners, then parts, then objects. Each layer's filters read the maps of the one below.
