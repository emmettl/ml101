Lab 09 · Scale

Scale Ladder

Pick a size for a model and the lab works out what follows: how much text it should read, how much arithmetic that takes, how long on how many chips, and how much memory just to hold it. These are the rules of thumb used to plan real training runs, and they fit on a postcard.

Scope: three standard approximations. Training arithmetic ≈ 6 × knobs × tokens. A well-fed model reads about 20 tokens per knob (Hoffmann et al., 2022). One modern accelerator does about 4 × 1014 useful operations a second once real-world inefficiency is allowed for. Figures for real models are those their makers published. Electricity, failed runs, data preparation, fine-tuning, the cost of answering questions afterwards and everything about closed models are left out.

Cost grows with the square

A bigger model needs more text, so both factors grow together. Ten times the model is a hundred times the bill. That single fact shapes the whole industry.

More chips buy time, not work

The arithmetic has to be done by someone. A thousand chips finish a thousand times sooner, for the same total. What they buy is a model this year instead of next century.

Text runs out before ambition does

The largest models already read more than any library holds. Finding enough good text, and not reading the same page twice, is now as much the constraint as chips.

Going further

The six-operations rule and the smooth fall of loss with scale are from Kaplan et al., “Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models” (2020). The twenty-tokens-per-knob rule is from Hoffmann et al., “Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models” (2022). GPT-3's figures are from Brown et al., “Language Models are Few-Shot Learners” (2020), and Llama 3.1's from Meta's “The Llama 3 Herd of Models” (2024). Learning from human preferences is described in Ouyang et al., “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback” (2022).
