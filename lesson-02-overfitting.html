Lesson 02 · Generalisation

Too clever by half

Give a machine enough knobs and it will fit its examples perfectly, and be wrong about everything else. The only honest score is on examples it has never seen.

The one idea

Memorising the examples is not the same as learning the pattern

A student who memorises last year's exam paper scores full marks on last year's paper. Set a new paper and you find out what they actually understood. Models are the same. The miss on the examples used for training always looks good, and always flatters. It tells you how well the knobs were turned. It does not tell you whether the machine will be right tomorrow.

So before training, set some examples aside and never let the model see them. After training, score it on those. That second number is the one that matters.

Training error tells you how hard the model tried. Held-out error tells you what it learned.
Capacity

How flexible the machine is: roughly, how many knobs it has. A line has two. The curve below can have thirteen.

Held-out data

Examples kept back from training and used only for scoring. Also called a test set.

Underfitting

Too stiff to follow the pattern. Bad on both scores.

Overfitting

Flexible enough to chase the noise. Superb on the training score, bad on the held-out one. See overfitting.

Why more knobs can be worse

The 14 solid points are training examples: a smooth wave plus noise. The hollow points are 200 held-out examples from the same source. The machine is a curve whose flexibility you control. At 1 it is a straight line. Each step up adds a knob and lets it bend once more.

At the top setting the curve passes almost exactly through all 14 training points, and between them it swings wildly. It has spent its flexibility reproducing the noise in those particular points. Noise does not repeat, so the next 200 points land nowhere near the swings.

The arithmetic

Three ways to buy back generalisation

Overfitting is a mismatch between how much the machine can express and how much the examples can pin down. Thirteen knobs and fourteen points leaves almost nothing to check the knobs against. There are three remedies, and real systems use all of them.

Fewer knobs

Choose the flexibility where the held-out miss is lowest, not the training miss.

More examples

With 30 points instead of 14, the same 13-knob curve's held-out miss falls from about 22 to about 0.12. Data is the most reliable cure there is.

A penalty on wildness

Add a charge to the loss for large knob values, so wiggling must pay for itself. This is regularisation.

And never peek

If you tune against the held-out set often enough, you start fitting it too. Keep a final set that is scored exactly once.

Takeaways

What to remember

Training error always flatters

It can only go down as capacity goes up. It cannot warn you.

Held-out error is U-shaped

Too stiff on the left, memorising on the right, and a sweet spot between.

Data beats cleverness

More examples tame a flexible model better than any trick.

This never goes away

A language model that recites its training text word for word is doing the same thing as the 13-knob curve.
