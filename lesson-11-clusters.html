Groups nobody labelled

Every model so far was shown the right answers and trained to match them. Most data in the world comes with no answers at all: customers, songs, cells, sentences, with nobody having said which belong together. Finding groups in that is called clustering, and its oldest method is a machine with k knobs that sets them by a loss it invented for itself.

The one idea

With no labels, the loss has to be made up

Lesson 00's machine had a loss because it had answers to miss. Take the answers away and there is nothing to be wrong about, so an unsupervised method has to decide for itself what a good answer looks like. K-means decides this: a good grouping is one where every point sits close to the centre of its group. Its loss is the total squared distance from points to centres, and its knobs are the positions of k centres.

It lowers that loss by alternating two steps, each of which can only bring it down. Give every point to its nearest centre. Move every centre to the middle of its points. Repeat until nothing moves. No gradient is computed and none is needed: this is descent by a different route, and like descent it can stop in a dip that is not the bottom.

K-means: guess k centres, assign each point to the nearest, move each centre to the middle of its points, repeat. The loss it shrinks is its own invention, and the k is yours.
Unsupervised

No labels, so no held-out score, no accuracy, no “right”. What you can measure is only what the method chose to measure.

Centres and fences

Nearest-centre means the fence between two clusters is a straight line halfway between them. All k-means groupings are cut from straight fences.

Starts matter

Different starting centres can settle in different places. The usual fix is to start far apart and to run several times, keeping the lowest loss.

The same vectors

Lesson 05's embeddings are points in a space. Cluster them and you get groups of words, songs or documents that nobody labelled either.

On the rings the loss settles contentedly at a grouping that has nothing to do with the rings, because a ring has no centre of its own that a straight fence could protect. On the stripes the fence cuts across them. K-means finds round groups of similar size, and nothing else, however long it runs. Other methods find other shapes, and each has a shape it cannot find.

The arithmetic, and what it does not buy

The loss cannot choose k for you

Add a centre and the loss falls, always, because every point is now at least as near to a centre as before. With as many centres as points the loss is zero and the grouping is worthless. So the number that k-means shrinks is silent on the question that matters most. The habit is to plot the settled loss against k and look for a bend, the “elbow”, where adding centres stops paying. On three clean round groups the bend is obvious. On most real data it is a matter of opinion, and the honest answer to “how many groups?” is “what are the groups for?”.

Each round costs one distance per point per centre, so k-means runs on millions of points where fancier methods cannot, which is much of why it survives.

Takeaways

What to remember

No labels, invented loss

An unsupervised method decides for itself what good looks like. Its score is that decision, not the truth.

Two steps, always downhill

Assign, then move. Each step lowers the loss, so it settles, possibly in the wrong dip. Start several times.

Straight fences, round groups

Nearest-centre draws straight boundaries. Rings and stripes need another method; every method has a shape it misses.

k is your decision

The loss always prefers more. The elbow is a habit. The purpose of the groups is the real guide.
