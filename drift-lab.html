Lab 13 · After launch

Drift Lab

Training stops. The world does not. A model that predicts which customers will cancel is trained on six months of data and then left to run for three years. Sometimes the customers change; sometimes the reason they cancel changes. Only one of those shows up on a monitor, and the labels that would show the other arrive months late.

Scope: the customers are invented, so the lab knows the true rule in every month and can score the best any model could have done. A real team cannot: it sees inputs at once, labels late, and the truth never. The model is the neuron of lesson 03 with three inputs. Every retraining uses the last six months of labelled data and needs six new months of it, which is one plausible rule among many. The scenarios are drawn simply; real drift is several of them at once, at different speeds.

Two kinds of change

When the customers change, the inputs move and a monitor sees it, but a model that learned the right rule keeps applying it. When the reason changes, the inputs look the same as ever and the rule quietly stops being true. The second is the one that hurts, and the one no input monitor can see.

Labels are the only real check, and they are late

Whether a prediction was right is known only once the customer has stayed or gone. Every accuracy figure in production is a figure about the past. Retrain on those labels and you may learn a world that has already ended.

Shipping is the start of the job

Lesson 02's held-out test protects you from a model that memorised. Nothing protects you from a world that moved except watching it. Retraining on a schedule is dull, and it is what most teams end up doing.

Going further

The split into changing inputs and a changing rule follows Gama et al., “A Survey on Concept Drift Adaptation” (2014). Sculley et al., “Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems” (2015), is the paper most teams read after their first model rots in production. Chip Huyen's “Data Distribution Shifts and Monitoring” is a practical account of the monitors and their limits.
