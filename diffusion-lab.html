Diffusion Lab

Six hundred points on a shape are drowned in noise, a little at a time, until nothing of the shape remains. A small network is trained on one job: given a drowned point and how far along the drowning it is, guess the noise. Then start from pure noise, remove the guessed noise sixty times over, and watch the shape come out. This is how picture generators work, with pixels in place of points.

Scope: two numbers per point instead of a million pixels, and a network of a few thousand knobs trained in your browser for a few seconds, so the drawings are rough where a product's would be sharp. Every piece is the real one: the noise schedule, the training target, the sampler. The faint dots are the true shape, shown so you can judge the drawing; the sampler never sees them. Real generators add a text encoder so that words can steer the drawing, which this lab omits.

Training

How well does it guess the noise?

The squared error between the noise added and the noise guessed, averaged over each 250 steps. Guessing zero every time would score 1.0. It never reaches zero, because early in the drowning the noise is genuinely unguessable.

Learn to remove, not to make

The network is never asked to draw a shape. It is asked, over and over, how much noise is in this point. Drawing is that skill run backwards, and a network that can clean a little can be made to clean a lot, one step at a time.

Many small steps beat one big one

From pure noise there is no right answer, only a direction. Taking a small step, adding a little fresh noise and asking again lets the cloud commit gradually, which is why the early steps look aimless and the shape appears late.

The same knobs, at any scale

Two numbers a point here; three per pixel, a million pixels, and a network of a billion knobs in a product. The drowning, the target and the sampler are unchanged. So is the descent that trains it.

Going further

The method is Ho, Jain and Abbeel, “Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models” (2020), whose training and sampling loops this lab follows with its noise schedule scaled to sixty steps. The idea of drowning data in noise and learning to reverse it is Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015). Products such as Stable Diffusion add a compressor so the drowning happens in a smaller space, and a text encoder so that a caption steers the drawing: Rombach et al., “High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models” (2022).
