Cluster Lab

Points on a plane and no labels at all. K-means guesses where k groups are, gives every point to its nearest centre, moves each centre to the middle of its points, and repeats until nothing moves. Step through the rounds, watch the one number it shrinks, and try it on the shapes it cannot find.

Scope: the points are invented in known groups, which is the only reason the lab can report how well the found clusters agree with them. Real data has no such answer key, and every score you can compute without one, the loss included, keeps falling as k rises. Two inputs so that the picture can be drawn; k-means works the same way in a thousand, where nothing can be drawn and the same limits apply.

What is it shrinking?

The total squared distance from every point to its centre, after each round. Both steps of a round can only lower it, which is why it always settles and why it can settle in the wrong place.

Choosing k

How many groups are there?

The settled loss for every k from 1 to 8, best of five starts. It falls at every step and always will. People look for the bend, the “elbow”, and call that k.

No labels, so no truth to score against

Every earlier lab had an answer to compare with. Here there is only a number the algorithm chose to shrink. It will shrink it whatever the data looks like, and a low loss is not the same as a grouping that means anything.

Round groups, straight fences

Nearest-centre means every boundary is a straight line halfway between two centres. Rings, stripes and groups of very different spread are cut wrongly, however many rounds are played. Other methods exist for those; none is free of assumptions.

k is your decision

The loss cannot choose k, because it always prefers more. The elbow is a habit, not a theorem. Run from several starts, keep the lowest loss, and then decide what the groups are for.

Going further

The algorithm is Lloyd's (1957, published 1982), “Least squares quantization in PCM”; the “spread out” start is Arthur and Vassilvitskii, “k-means++: the advantages of careful seeding” (2007). For the shapes k-means cannot find, DBSCAN (Ester et al., 1996) and spectral clustering are the usual next steps; the scikit-learn clustering guide shows them side by side on exactly these kinds of puzzle.
